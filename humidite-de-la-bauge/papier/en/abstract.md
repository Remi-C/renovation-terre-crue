# Moisture in cob — abstract

Rémi Cura, PhD — Clos Poulain, near Rennes — November 2026

**The question.** Does an old cob wall, dry to the touch, hold water, how much, and where? Few measurements exist in old
cob in service, and it is the starting state of any moisture calculation before insulating. We took earth from three
walls of a cob house near Rennes (17 November 2026), and weighed each sample as taken and after drying in a kitchen oven
(18 November), with ordinary equipment.

**What we found.** A dry core and a wet skin: in the wall of an unheated, very well ventilated garden shed, the first 10 cm
hold 10.1 ± 0.3 % water (by dry mass of earth), the next 10 cm 1.7 %; the attic partition wall is dry (1.4 and 1.8 %). The
façade cores gave too little earth (0.5 to 2 g per slice) for a profile; pooled (0 to 20 cm), 5.3 ± 1.6 %, at the top of
what the literature calls normal at the base of a cob wall (3 to 5 %). Sampling biases (stones, heating while drilling)
make the earth look drier than it is.

**What this changes for insulating.** A water content is not a relative humidity: going from one to the other needs the
sorption isotherm of the wall's earth, not measured here. On two published laboratory earths, the façade is worth 87 to
97 % relative humidity; the same 3 % content is worth 75 % on one, 90 % on the other; and between 80 and 90 % the water
content moves by only half a point. A damp skin of 10 cm amounts to some 12 to 15 kg of water per m² of wall, which will
have to leave, and it must be measured when the wall is closed. The author's moisture calculations for specific insulation
systems are not published: the paper draws no conclusion about any insulant.
