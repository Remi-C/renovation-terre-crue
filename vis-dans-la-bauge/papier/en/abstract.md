# Screws in cob — abstract

Rémi Cura, PhD — Clos Poulain, near Rennes — November 2026

**What.** The guides for earth building accept a wood screw driven directly into cob (French *bauge*), for light
loads, but say neither how much it holds nor with what diameter. We found no measurement of a screw installed
afterwards in earth and loaded sideways. We ran eight tests in an old, dry cob wall: off-the-shelf screws (fischer
PowerFull II of 8 and 10 mm, a 6 mm stainless steel screw), each clamping a board against the wall, pulled along the
wall with a crane scale. One wall, one day, one test per configuration: these are not professional tests, but they are
remarkably consistent with one another.

**How much.** A 10 mm screw, driven with a pilot hole and 9.5 to 21.5 cm into the cob, carried 300 to 350 kg sideways
without failing (tests stopped before failure). On its own, without a board, the same screw also carried 300 kg, but
while moving by several millimetres. Too short, the screws gave way: at about 200 kg for a 10 mm screw 5.5 cm in and an
8 mm screw 7.5 cm in, from 50 kg for a small stainless steel screw 3.5 cm in.

**What to do** (the author's advice, for this dry cob and short-term loads): 8 mm for a few tens of kilos, 10 mm above
40 to 50 kg per screw; 8 cm into the cob at the very least, 10 cm preferably; always clamp a piece against the wall; no
need for a pilot hole for an ordinary 6 mm screw, under light loads; for a 10 mm screw, a pilot hole: without one, the
drill-driver stalled at 18–20 N·m at about 10 cm of cob and driving was very hard; with a 5 or 6 mm pilot hole, almost
everything was driven at 20 N·m at most, down to 21.5 cm. The pilot hole clearly reduces the driving torque and makes
it possible to stay within the manufacturer's recommendations; which pilot hole for which diameter and which depth
remains to be measured. Many modest screws rather than a few heavily loaded ones. The paper does not choose a margin:
it lists those used elsewhere (from 1.25 to 14 depending on what they apply to). Pull-out remains to be measured: it is
the subject of a second campaign, in November 2026.
